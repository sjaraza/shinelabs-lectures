Meet your robot

Pick it up. Turn it over.

  • How many sensors can you find?
  • How many things can move?
  • Which part is the computer?
  • Where does the power come from?

Two minutes. Compare answers with the person next to you.

Do not answer for them — let them hunt. The next four slides confirm what they found, which lands far better than being told first. Expected answers: three sensors (ultrasonic, 3-channel grayscale, camera), five things that move (two drive motors, steering servo, camera pan, camera tilt), the computer is the small board underneath, power is the two-cell pack. Walk the room while they do this — it is also your chance to spot a robot that is assembled wrongly before it matters.

Wi-Fi2.4 GHz only. This is why the robots and your laptops use different networks

StorageThe microSD card is the hard drive
512 MB of RAM That is roughly one-thirtieth of a modern laptop. It shapes everything we do — and it is a real engineering constraint, not a toy one.
Worth dwelling on the RAM figure. It explains later design decisions, like why the dashboard fetches a single photo instead of streaming video.

The brain-board: Robot HAT v4

The Pi has no analogue inputs and cannot drive a motor. This board adds both.

ProvidesDetail
Motor driver2 channels — direction pin + speed (PWM) each
Servo outputs12 channels; we use P0 P1 P2
Analogue inputsA0–A3, 12-bit — the Pi alone has none
Battery sensingA4, wired internally to the pack
SpeakerMono, over I²S
The useful idea here: a microcontroller is good at precise timing and analogue, a Linux computer is good at everything else. Real robots layer them exactly like this.

What moves

ActuatorRangeJob
2 × drive motors−100 … +100rear wheels, forward and back
Steering servo±30°front wheels
Camera pan servo±90°look left and right
Camera tilt servo−35° … +65°look up and down

Steering only reaches ±30°, not ±90°. Ask it to turn further and it simply won't — a limit written into the software to protect the linkage.

Good moment to ask why a steering servo would be deliberately limited in software. Answer: because the mechanical linkage binds beyond that, and stalling a servo draws current and cooks it.

What senses

SensorReadsUsed for
Ultrasonicdistance in cm“is something in front of me?”
Grayscale ×33 × brightness, 0–4095following a line, spotting a cliff edge
Camera (ov5647)5 MP stillseverything in lectures 2 and 3
Sensors lie The ultrasonic returns -1 when it hears nothing back — not “minus one centimetre”. Handling that honestly is most of what robotics software actually is.
This is the single most important idea in the whole session. Every sensor has a failure mode, and pretending otherwise is how robots drive into walls. It sets up lecture 1 directly.

Looking after the battery

Pack2 × 18650 Li-ion, 2000 mAh
Full8.4 V
Protection cutoff6.0 V

The two LEDs tell you everything

●● both litabove 7.6 V — fine
● one litabove 7.15 V — finish up
○ none litstop and charge
Never run it flat Draining a lithium pack below its cutoff damages it permanently. If both LEDs are out, put the robot down and tell me — don't push on.
Say this early and mean it. Batteries are charged between sessions; a pack murdered in lecture 0 is a robot missing from lecture 1.

How your robot gets online

There is a private Wi-Fi network in this room.

Robots joinShineLabs — 2.4 GHz
Your laptop joinsPLACEHOLDER — 5 GHz SSID
Passwordson the board — not in the slides

Both networks are the same network underneath, so your laptop can reach your robot. Robots get the 2.4 GHz band to themselves because that is all a Pi Zero 2 W can use.

Deliberately do NOT explain how the network is built. It is not today's lesson and it invites twenty tangents. "There is a network, here is how you join it" is enough.

First: name your robot

Give it a name you like. Write it on the sticker and put it on your robot. 

zoomer      pixel
wall-e      taco
sir-rolls   nova

Rules: lower case, no spaces, letters and numbers, hyphens are fine. Keep it short — you'll type it a lot.

This name matters You'll type it into the SD-card setup in a moment, and then use it every session to reach your robot — if you call it zoomer, its address is zoomer.local

Two people can't have the same name. Shout yours out so we don't clash.

Do the physical labelling now, before any software. The sticker and the hostname must match or nothing else today makes sense — a robot with no label is the single most common source of confusion in the room. Creative names are good for engagement but need policing on two fronts. First, duplicates: have them call the name out, and keep a written roster of student → robot name as you go. You will need that roster all series, since you can no longer guess what anyone's robot is called. Second, hostname legality: lower case, letters, digits and hyphens only. No spaces, no underscores, no emoji, must not start or end with a hyphen. If somebody insists on "Mr. Roboto", steer them to "mr-roboto". My own robot is robot0 — a dull name is a fine example of the format.

Installing the OS

Two things people get wrong Pick 64-bit, not 32-bit — some of our libraries don't exist for 32-bit. And check you selected the SD card, not your laptop's own disk, before writing.
Imaging is meant to be done at home. This slide is the catch-up path — keep it visible while stragglers work, and pair them with someone who is done. Two failure modes to watch for. First, students who clicked SKIP CUSTOMISATION — then there is no hostname, no Wi-Fi and no SSH, and the card must be rewritten. Second, a hostname that does not match their sticker. Tell them explicitly to write the username and password down. Somebody always forgets what they typed. Keep 2–3 spare pre-imaged cards in your bag for anyone truly stuck.

Boot it

  1. Card into the slot underneath the Pi
  2. Battery connected, switch on at the Robot HAT
  3. Wait — the first boot takes longer than later ones
Checkpoint 1 ping zoomer.local gets a reply — using your robot's name.
First boot expands the filesystem and joins Wi-Fi, so give it a couple of minutes before declaring it broken. If ping fails: wrong Wi-Fi details in Imager is the usual cause, and it means rewriting the card. Check the hostname spelling before assuming that, though.

Now your laptop needs a terminal

Two different machines, two different jobs.

On the robotSSH server — that was the “Enable SSH” tick in Imager
On your laptopSSH client — the ssh command you're about to type

Nearly every laptop already has the client. Check with: 

ssh -V

A version number means you're ready. An error means the next slide.

Worth separating these two explicitly — students conflate "SSH is enabled" on the robot with having a client on their laptop, and then cannot tell which end is at fault. `ssh -V` prints to stderr, so it looks slightly odd but is fine.

Where's my terminal?

macOS

Terminal — ⌘Space, type “Terminal”.
ssh is already installed. Nothing to do.

Linux

Any terminal app. If ssh is missing: 

sudo apt install openssh-client

Windows 10 / 11

Open Windows Terminal or PowerShell from the Start menu. ssh is included on current Windows.

If ssh -V is not recognised, add it once:

Settings → System → Optional features → Add a feature → OpenSSH Client → Install

Then close and reopen the terminal.

In practice: macOS and Linux always work. Windows 10 build 1803 and later ship the OpenSSH client enabled, so most Windows students are fine too — the Optional features route is the rare fallback. Do not send anyone to PuTTY. It is another download, another interface to explain, and the built-in client is right there. If a Windows student has an old build and no admin rights, pair them with someone until you can sort it out after class.

Log in to your robot

It's running. Now get a prompt on it.

Use your robot's name and the username you chose when you set up the card. 

ssh yourname@zoomer.local
Checkpoint 2 Your prompt changed to yourname@zoomer. You are now typing on the robot, not on your laptop.
Make the shift explicit — students routinely lose track of which machine they are typing on for the rest of the session. Ask them to run `hostname` to prove it. Scan the room before moving on. Two predictable snags: the username is whatever they typed into Imager, not their real name and not mine; and the first connection asks "Are you sure you want to continue connecting?" — the answer is yes.

What is SSH, actually?

An encrypted remote shell. You type on your laptop; the commands run on the robot; the output comes back.

It is how nearly every server on the internet is administered, and it is the same tool professionals use daily. 

hostname
zoomer
uptime
 up 3 min
exit
Worth thirty seconds. It demystifies the thing they will use all series, and "this is what real engineers use" lands well with this age group.

Read your robot's vitals

ReadingWhat it tells you
Battery voltagehow much driving you have left
CPU temperaturewhether it is working hard or overheating
Wi-Fi signalcloser to 0 is better; −38 dBm is excellent
Distancecentimetres to the nearest object, or -1
Grayscale ×30–4095 per sensor — darker surface, lower number

Wave your hand in front of the ultrasonic sensor and watch the number move. Then cover it completely and watch what happens.

The "cover it completely" experiment is the point — they will see -1 and understand viscerally why sensor error handling matters. Let them discover it rather than telling them.

Drive it

Before you touch the controls Robot on the floor, not the desk. Wheels clear. Nothing in front of it. A robot that drives off a table lands on its camera.
Enforce this properly — pause until every robot is on the floor. Twenty students, twenty first-time drivers, and desks are exactly 75 cm high.

Steering and speed

Speed goes from 0 straight to about half power — there is no gentle crawl. That is a property of the motor driver, not a bug in your dashboard.

Checkpoint 5 Drive forward a metre, turn, and come back. Then stop it.
The absent slow-speed range is genuinely interesting: the library remaps any non-zero speed to at least 50% duty cycle. Sets up lecture 1's "controlling a wheeled robot efficiently" nicely.

Aim the camera, take a picture

  1. Pan and tilt until the camera points where you want
  2. Press Capture
  3. Wait — it takes a second or two to expose properly
Checkpoint 6 A photograph, taken by your robot, on your screen.

A single still, not video. On 512 MB of RAM, streaming video would cost more than it is worth — an engineering trade-off, made on purpose.

Good closing activity: it is visual, it is shareable, and it is a natural hook into lecture 2. Encourage them to post pictures in Slack.
