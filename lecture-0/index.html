Worth dwelling on the RAM figure. It explains later design decisions, like why the dashboard fetches a single photo instead of streaming video.

The brain-board: Robot HAT v4

The Pi has no analogue inputs and cannot drive a motor. This board adds both.

ProvidesDetail
Motor driver2 channels — direction pin + speed (PWM) each
Servo outputs12 channels; we use P0 P1 P2
Analogue inputsA0–A3, 12-bit — the Pi alone has none
Battery sensingA4, wired internally to the pack
SpeakerMono, over I²S
The useful idea here: a microcontroller is good at precise timing and analogue, a Linux computer is good at everything else. Real robots layer them exactly like this.

What moves

ActuatorRangeJob
2 × drive motors−100 … +100rear wheels, forward and back
Steering servo±30°front wheels
Camera pan servo±90°look left and right
Camera tilt servo−35° … +65°look up and down

Steering only reaches ±30°, not ±90°. Ask it to turn further and it simply won't — a limit written into the software to protect the linkage.

Good moment to ask why a steering servo would be deliberately limited in software. Answer: because the mechanical linkage binds beyond that, and stalling a servo draws current and cooks it.

What senses

SensorReadsUsed for
Ultrasonicdistance in cm“is something in front of me?”
Grayscale ×33 × brightness, 0–4095following a line, spotting a cliff edge
Camera (ov5647)5 MP stillseverything in lectures 2 and 3
Sensors lie The ultrasonic returns -1 when it hears nothing back — not “minus one centimetre”. Handling that honestly is most of what robotics software actually is.
This is the single most important idea in the whole session. Every sensor has a failure mode, and pretending otherwise is how robots drive into walls. It sets up lecture 1 directly.

Looking after the battery

Pack2 × 18650 Li-ion, 2000 mAh
Full8.4 V
Protection cutoff6.0 V

The two LEDs tell you everything

●● both litabove 7.6 V — fine
● one litabove 7.15 V — finish up
○ none litstop and charge
Never run it flat Draining a lithium pack below its cutoff damages it permanently. If both LEDs are out, put the robot down and tell me — don't push on.
Say this early and mean it. Batteries are charged between sessions; a pack murdered in lecture 0 is a robot missing from lecture 1.

Both networks are the same network underneath, so your laptop can reach your robot. Robots get the 2.4 GHz band to themselves because that is all a Pi Zero 2 W can use.

Deliberately do NOT explain how the network is built. It is not today's lesson and it invites twenty tangents. "There is a network, here is how you join it" is enough.

First contact

Power on. Find it. Log in.

  1. Flip the switch on the Robot HAT
  2. Wait for the LEDs — give it about 40 seconds to boot
  3. Check the label on your robot for its name
Expect the first real stall here: robots that were not switched on, or whose battery is flat. Have a couple of charged spare packs to hand.

Say hello

Replace 0 with your robot's number.

ping robot0.local
ssh ali@robot0.local
Checkpoint 1 Your prompt says ali@robot0. You are now typing on the robot, not on your laptop.
Make the shift explicit — students routinely lose track of which machine they are typing on for the rest of the session. Ask them to run `hostname` to prove it. Scan the room before moving on.

What is SSH, actually?

An encrypted remote shell. You type on your laptop; the commands run on the robot; the output comes back.

It is how nearly every server on the internet is administered, and it is the same tool professionals use daily. 

hostname
robot0
uptime
 up 3 min
exit
Worth thirty seconds. It demystifies the thing they will use all series, and "this is what real engineers use" lands well with this age group.

Read your robot's vitals

ReadingWhat it tells you
Battery voltagehow much driving you have left
CPU temperaturewhether it is working hard or overheating
Wi-Fi signalcloser to 0 is better; −38 dBm is excellent
Distancecentimetres to the nearest object, or -1
Grayscale ×30–4095 per sensor — darker surface, lower number

Wave your hand in front of the ultrasonic sensor and watch the number move. Then cover it completely and watch what happens.

The "cover it completely" experiment is the point — they will see -1 and understand viscerally why sensor error handling matters. Let them discover it rather than telling them.

Drive it

Before you touch the controls Robot on the floor, not the desk. Wheels clear. Nothing in front of it. A robot that drives off a table lands on its camera.
Enforce this properly — pause until every robot is on the floor. Twenty students, twenty first-time drivers, and desks are exactly 75 cm high.

Steering and speed

Speed goes from 0 straight to about half power — there is no gentle crawl. That is a property of the motor driver, not a bug in your dashboard.

Checkpoint 4 Drive forward a metre, turn, and come back. Then stop it.
The absent slow-speed range is genuinely interesting: the library remaps any non-zero speed to at least 50% duty cycle. Sets up lecture 1's "controlling a wheeled robot efficiently" nicely.

Aim the camera, take a picture

  1. Pan and tilt until the camera points where you want
  2. Press Capture
  3. Wait — it takes a second or two to expose properly
Checkpoint 5 A photograph, taken by your robot, on your screen.

A single still, not video. On 512 MB of RAM, streaming video would cost more than it is worth — an engineering trade-off, made on purpose.

Good closing activity: it is visual, it is shareable, and it is a natural hook into lecture 2. Encourage them to post pictures in Slack.
